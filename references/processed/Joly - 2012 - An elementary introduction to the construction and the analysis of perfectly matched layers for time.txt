As soon as people have been interested in the numerical simulation of wave propa-
gation phenomena, particularly in the time domain, they had to face the delicate ques-
tion of realizing “transparent” artiﬁcial boundaries. Indeed, many problems of this type
are posed in a unbounded domain (or in a domain which is very large with respect to
a zone of interest in which one wishes to observe the solution) : one thus has to bound
artiﬁcially the computational domain in such a way that the non physical boundaries
are “transparent” for the solution. Due to the crucial importance of this subject, but
also because of its difﬁculty, this question has aroused the curiosity from many re-
searchers, ﬁrst from engineers, next from applied mathematicians. It would be much
too ambitious to give a complete panorama of a research that began almost forty years
ago (see however the article by T. Hagstrm [20] which constitutes a quite complete
review of the domain) but one can now measure how much the many ingenious ideas
proposed by the one or the others have permitted to accomplish impressive progress
in this matter. Nowadays, if one restricts oneself to linear wave propagation problems,
one can consider that, even though some questions remain open, satisfactory solutions
are available for a large number of applications, in particular in the “canonical case” I
will have in mind throughout this paper, namely the case where one wishes to reduce
numerical computations to a convex bounded domain, assuming that the exterior do-
main is homogeneous and source free.

Recently, in particular for the applications in acoustics and electromagnetism, impor-
tant progress has been achieved in the use of “exact” boundary conditions associated
to homogeneous exterior domains. One usually introduces a boundary operator called
“Dirichlet to Neumann” (DtN) operator that can be represented by using separation of
variables in the exterior domain (in 3D, one will use typically spherical harmonics [19],
which requires a spherical artiﬁcial boundary) or through the coupling with an integral
representation formula of the exterior solution (which relies on the explicit knowledge
of the Green’s function of the problem) [4].

However, these techniques, beyond the fact that they remain restricted to a relatively
limited ﬁeld of applications, have the disadvantage to be nonlocal in space and time
: they remain quite difﬁcult to implement and rather costly in terms of computational
time and storage, despite of the recent progress accomplished is fast algorithms for the
evaluation of such nonlocal operators, such as the multipole method [18]. That is why,
in many computational codes, one prefers to use solutions involving local operators,
typically differential operators, which lead to a much simpler implementation. Such
solutions, except in 1D, can not provide transparent boundary conditions : one speaks
of ”approximate” or ”absorbing” boundary conditions. Of course, these conditions in-
volve approximation parameters (devoted to tend to 0 or +∞) and will provide the true
solution only when ”passing to the limit”. In this class of methods, two large families
of methods can be opposed:

• Absorbing boundary conditions (ABC’s): The idea is to complete the interior
mathematical model (namely the interior PDE’s) with one or several boundary
conditions that can be seen as partial differential equations along the boundary.
Such conditions are supposed to minimize the spurious reﬂections that they
generate. The pioneering works of Engquist and Majda [15], [16] on the wave
equation (in the half space, i. e. with a ﬂat artiﬁcial boundary) are in this spirit.
They proposed a hierarchy of boundary conditions that are classiﬁed with an
integer N , namely the order of the boundary condition that I will not precisely
deﬁne here. Let us simply explain that the greater is N , the higher is the order of
the differential operators (which means that the boundary condition is more and
more costly in terms of computational time) and the better is the accuracy of the
approximate solution, as it has been recently proven in [14] for instance. These
initial works have led to an intensive research in applied mathematics aiming
at extending this type of techniques to other equations, the treatment of curved
boundaries, the treatment of corners,...

• Absorbing layers : the idea here is that the absorption of the waves that ”want”
to leave the computational domain is not achieved through a boundary condition
but inside a (hopefully small) layer surrounding this computational domain. This
layer will be made of an ”absorbing material” whose behavior remains described
with the help of partial differential equations and inside which the waves are
damped (hopefully exponentially fast) during their propagation. In such a case,
the boundary condition that has to be prescribed at the external boundary of the
absorbing layer has no particular importance as soon as it leads to a well posed
problem...

The idea, physically rather natural, of absorbing layers has ﬁrst been introduced by
engineers at the beginning of the 70’s. The idea was to join the physical non absorbing
domain to an absorbing medium of physical nature (typically, in electromagnetism one
will use a conductive material, in mechanics a visco-elastic material, ...). This approach
has been progressively abandoned - during the 80’s - at the beneﬁt of absorbing
boundary conditions for various reasons, in particular because it was not supported
- contrary to ABC’s - by a solid and precise theoretical background but also because
its practical use required what I will cal some ”engineer intuition”. In the mid 90’s, the

works of J. P. B´erenger [8], [9], who, for Maxwell’s equations, invented the concept
of Perfectly Matched Layers (PMLs), have revolutionized the domain and replaced the
methods of absorbing layers as a very attractive and powerful alternative to absorbing
boundary conditions. The success of PMLs, whose principle is based on a simple but
very clever idea, can be explained by several good reasons among which

• the fact that PML’s are particularly simple to implement (in particular by

comparison with higher order absorbing boundary conditions),

• the fact that they provide in many applications remarkable performances (even

when the theory does not completely predict it),

• the fact that they can be applied without extreme additional complexity to a great
number of equations (including acoustics, electromagnetism, elastodynamics,...)

However, to temperate any excessive enthusiasm, I will add that

• although substantial progress have been accomplished during the past ten years
(which I will try to show later) the mathematical analysis of these methods is
still not sufﬁciently well understood (at least from my point of view).

• there exist physical models for which they do not work because they lead to

unstable mathematical models.

• the winner of the competition PMLs agains ABCs is not clearly designated yet

and a fair comparison remains to be done.

As many (really many) things have already been written on PML’s, I will choose to
insist in this text on a particular point of view, which I hope to be well adapted to a
reader who is an applied mathematician. More precisely, I will pursue a double goal:

• Give a very introductory exposition of the construction of PML’s, which is
not the one originally the one of B´erenger but better corresponds to my
understanding of the method,

• Give a kind of state of the art in the matter of mathematical results about PML’s,
with a particular emphasis on the works done by my collaborators and myself at
INRIA.

In this spirit, I have chosen to organize the content as follows. Chapter 1 concerns
the 1D case and has essentially a pedagogical motivation. After a brief introduction
on classical absorbing layers, I will present - starting from the classical concept of
impedance adaptation - the construction of PML’s for the 1D wave equation. In this
very simple case, everything becomes quite simple and can be analyzed ”by hand”.
Chapter 2 is devoted to B´erenger’s PML’s for a general hyperbolic system in dimension
2 (once one has understood the two-dimensional case, the extension to higher space
dimensions does not pose any problem). I will show how, from a very mathematical
and formal idea - the use of a ”complex” (in the sense of imaginary numbers) change
of variable - one can derive PML’s that generalize the ones proposed by B´erenger for
Maxwell’s equations. In a second step, I will restrict myself to the 2D acoustic wave

1.2 The Perfectly Matched Layers in 1D.

The main drawback of the layers studied in the previous section is the reﬂection of
waves at the interface between the real medium and the absorbing one. This raises
the natural question: can one conceive an absorbing layer which would be ”perfectly
matched”, that is to say that would generate no reﬂection at the interface with the
physical medium ? If we restrict ourselves to the type of absorbing layer considered in
the previous paragraph, the answer is no, even if ν et ν∗ are authorized to vary in x.
However, we shall see that the answer becomes positive if one authorizes non physical
absorption mechanisms.

3 Mathematical questions

Up to now, we did not attack the purely theoretical questions linked to PMLs. As
soon as these techniques have appeared, many researchers have been interested in
their mathematical analysis. A ﬁrst article by Abarbanel and Gottlieb [1] (claiming
the the PML model in electromagnetism was ”ill-posed”, I will come back on this)
provoked many interrogations (in particular because their statement seemed to be in
contradiction with numerical observations). Since then, much progress has been made
in understanding the theoretical properties of PMLs. This chapter will treat some
aspects of this topic.

The ﬁrst natural question is : does the PML model give rise to a well-posed evolution
problem ? The same question was posed of course for absorbing boundary conditions
and the answer was far from obvious (see remark 11). In what follows, we shall be
interested in the Cauchy problem associated the system (29) (for simplicity, we restrict
ourselves to the PML model ”in the x direction”). At least, we would like to investigate
the well-posedness issue in the very simple situation where σ is constant and non zero
in all IR2

distinct aspects (I insist on this because the distinction is not always done properly in
the literature, where one there is sometimes confusion between the words ”ill-posed”

3.3 On the stability of PML models.

We are going to see in this section that the PML models that we have presented
are not always stable. More precisely, we are going to give a condition on the
original propagation model which is necessary for the stability of the PML model .This
condition is related to the shape of the ”wavefronts” in the original system. It is not
my purpose to give here the rigorous deﬁnition of a wavefront. Roughly speaking,
in 2D, the wavefronts are curves which describe the ”shape” of a wave emitted from
a point source. More rigorously, these curves permit to describe the location of the
singularities of this solution, at a given time. Which is important for what follows is
that wavefronts can be characterized geometrically in terms of the so-called slowness
curves which are themselves deduced from the dispersion relation of the propagation
model.
