A Nonconvolutional, Split-Field, Perfectly Matched Layer for
Wave Propagation in Isotropic and Anisotropic
Elastic Media: Stability Analysis

by Kristel C. Meza-Fajardo and Apostolos S. Papageorgiou

Abstract A nonconvolutional, split-field, perfectly matched layer, referred to as
the multiaxial perfectly matched layer (M-PML), is proposed and implemented.
The new formulation is obtained by generalizing the classical perfectly matched layer
(PML; as originally proposed by Bérenger, [1994]) to a medium in which damping
profiles are specified in more than one direction. Under the hypothesis of small damp-
ing and using an eigenvalue sensitivity analysis based on first derivatives, we propose
a method to study the stability of the M-PML. With this method we demonstrate that
the stability of the M-PML is related to the ratios of the specified damping profiles.
Recognition of this fact leads to a general procedure for constructing robust, stable
M-PML models for anisotropic media. It is also demonstrated that for any anisotropic
medium the classical PML exhibits instabilities related to an eigenvalue with zero real
part of multiplicity higher than one. Furthermore, we show that exponential growth
due to eigenvalues with positive real part can be present in the classical PML for some
orthotropic media. The effectiveness of the proposed M-PML and its advantages re-
lative to the classical PML are demonstrated by constructing stable terminations for the
aforementioned anisotropic media. The method of stability analysis is developed and
demonstrated for two-dimensional elastodynamics problems, but its extension to
three-dimensional configurations is straightforward.

Introduction

The perfectly matched layer (PML) model was intro-
duced by Bérenger (1994) as a material boundary condition
for electromagnetic wave propagation problems in un-
bounded domains. The model consists of an absorbing layer
of finite width that surrounds the physical domain of interest
so that all outgoing waves are damped out irrespective of
their frequency and direction of propagation. The main fea-
ture of the PML is that, in the case of the continuum (i.e.,
before discretization), it does not generate reflections at
the interface separating the absorbing layer and the physical
medium (i.e., it is perfectly matched); however, a small re-
flection is always present after discretization. Being a mate-
rial absorbing condition, the PML leads naturally to sparse
systems; it is well suited for implementation in parallel
computers and, consequently, is very attractive for numerical
simulations. In its original version (i.e., as it was originally
proposed by Bérenger [1994], we refer to this version as
the classical PML), the field variables of the PML are split
into nonphysical components so as to make it possible to
incorporate in the mathematical formulation the desired ab-
sorption. This mathematical formulation of the PML has
come to be known as the split formulation. Because of

the effectiveness of the PML in absorbing the radiation ex-
iting the physical domain, the extra memory storage required
in order to accommodate the increased number of field
variables is usually counterbalanced by the savings that
are achieved by reducing the size of the physical domain.
The satisfactory (in most cases) performance of the PML
has resulted in a considerable amount of work toward its
implementation in several wavelike problems (a concise
yet comprehensive review of the subject is presented by
Komatitsch and Martin [2007]), such as Maxwell’s equations
(e.g., Bérenger, 1996, 1998; Collino and Monk, 1998;
Teixeira and Chew, 1998a), the linearized Euler equations
(e.g., Hu, 1996; Hesthaven, 1998; Nataf, 2006), Helmholtz
equations (e.g., Teixeira and Chew, 1998b; Harari et al.,
2000), and the elastic wave equations (e.g., Chew and
Liu, 1996; Hastings et al., 1996; Collino and Tsogka,
2001; Festa and Nielsen, 2003; Komatitsch and Tromp,
2003; Basu and Chopra, 2004).

Despite the general success of the PML in most ap-
plications, there are still some instances for which the per-
formance of the PML does not meet expectations. In the case
of elastic waves in isotropic media, it has been reported

(Festa et al., 2005; Komatitsch and Martin, 2007) that large
reflections are obtained for waves entering the PML at graz-
ing incidence and also that instabilities appear in long (in

in the fields of electromagnetism (De Moerloose and Stuchly,
1995; Bérenger, 1998) and acoustics (Hu, 1996). In addition,
Bécache et al. (2003) documented that exponentially grow-
ing solutions could appear in some models for anisotropic
media. These problems have been intensively investigated
in recent years, and a significant number of contributions
on the subject has appeared in the published literature.
For example, it was initially thought (Kuzuoglu and Mittra,
1996) that the source of the dynamical instability of the clas-
sical PML could be attributed to the fact the constitutive para-
meters did not satisfy causality, and an alternative causal
frequency-dependent PML, known as the convolutional per-
fectly matched layer (C-PML), was proposed. Later, Teixeira
and Chew (1999) showed that the original Cartesian PML
parameters do indeed satisfy causality and that the aforemen-
tioned conclusion (regarding causality) was reached based
on a technical error related to the Kramers–Kronig equations.
Another approach was taken by Abarbanel and Gottlieb
(1997) who suggested that because the split formulation is
only weakly well posed, the PML solution diverged under
small perturbations. This result motivated various investiga-
tors to develop unsplit formulations (Gedney, 1996; Zhao
and Cangellaris, 1996; Ziolkowski, 1997; Abarbanel and
Gottlieb, 1998; Basu and Chopra, 2004; Appelö and Kreiss,
2005). The unsplit formulations have the advantage of a
more straightforward implementation in the various numeri-
cal schemes, although they also imply an additional compu-
tational cost, in some cases comparable to that of the split
PML, due to the presence of either convolution operations,
high-order time derivatives, or auxiliary variables. It was
later found (Abarbanel et al., 2002) that the unsplit formula-
tions for Maxwell’s equations were still susceptible to
manifest slowly growing spurious solutions that eventually
spread and pollute the physical domain. Subsequently, it
was demonstrated (Bécache et al., 2004) that the C-PML does
not suffer from such instabilities. It was also reported that the
C-PML improves the absorption of waves at grazing inci-
dence (Roden and Gedney, 2000; Bérenger, 2002; Festa
and Vilotte, 2005; Komatitsch and Martin, 2007). However,
it should be noted that the C-PML behaves as an elastic
medium at low frequencies, losing its absorptive capacity.
Furthermore, at high frequencies C-PML reduces asymptoti-
cally to the PML, losing its stabilizing properties. In addition,
Komatitsch and Martin (2007) observed that the C-PML does
not solve all the instability problems of PML models for elas-
tic anisotropic media reported by Bécache et al. (2003).
More recently, Appelö and Kreiss (2005) succeeded in sta-
bilizing some but not all those PML models for anisotropic
media, by implementing a PML formulation obtained from a
modification of the modal solutions of the elastic system
(Hagstrom, 2003). Despite all the work done on the subject,
a comprehensive mathematical analysis of the PML is not yet

available, and the problem of developing a general method to
construct stable PML terminations remains open.

In this article, we extend the formulation of the split-
field PML to a more general absorptive medium, which
we refer to as multiaxial perfectly matched layer (M-PML),
for which damping profiles are specified along more than
one direction. We also present a simple method to demon-
strate that the stability of the system is related to the ratio
of the damping profiles and thus provide a general procedure
for constructing stable M-PML terminations for any model of
anisotropic elastic media. The method provides a split-field
M-PML for anisotropic elastodynamics that does not require
convolution operations and remains efficient and stable for
all frequencies and all directions of propagation. The analy-
sis is presented for two-dimensional inplane (P-SV) wave
propagation in elastic orthotropic media. However, extension
of the results to three dimensions is straightforward. The gen-
erality of the method renders it suitable to study stability of
PMLs for other wavelike problems (such as in acoustics and
electromagnetism) as well. An analytical and numerical
study of the accuracy of the M-PML for simulations using
the spectral element method has been performed and will
be reported in a future publication.

Stability Analysis

In this section we perform a stability analysis of the
M-PML for isotropic as well as orthotropic elastic media. In-
stabilities in PML implementations reported in the literature
have been detected using different discretization methods,
such as the finite differences method (Hu, 1996; Komatitsch
and Martin, 2007), the finite element method (Bécache et al.,
2003), and the spectral element method (Festa et al., 2005).
Therefore, the stability analysis is carried out for the conti-
nuum; that is, we study the stability of the solutions of the
partial differential equation that governs the motion of
the M-PML.

criteria provided by the theory of linear dynamical systems
(e.g., Hinrichsen and Pritchard, 2005; Velhurst, 2006). This

theory studies the stability of linear autonomous systems by
investigating the behavior of the solutions close to the equi-
librium points. An equilibrium point is a solution that is time
independent (i.e., a solution that satisfies ∂U=∂t (cid:1) 0). Note
that if matrix A in (37) is nonsingular, the only equilibrium
point of the autonomous system is the origin U(cid:2)t(cid:3) (cid:1) 0. Then,
one question that has to be addressed is whether solutions
that start to evolve at time t (cid:1) t0 in a neighborhood of
the origin will stay in this neighborhood for t > t0. A stron-
ger type of stability would require that solutions not only stay
in a neighborhood of the origin but also converge to it with
time. This is the notion of asymptotic stability. An equili-
brium point is called asymptotically stable if all solutions
U∈Rn approach the equilibrium point as t → ∞. Further-
more, as briefly outlined in the Appendix, the equilibrium
solution at the origin U(cid:2)t(cid:3) (cid:1) 0 is asymptotically stable if
all the eigenvalues fσig of A have negative real parts. For
the autonomous system (37), this means that all solutions
U(cid:2)t(cid:3) are composed of transients (i.e., terms that exponen-
tially decay to zero). When at least one eigenvalue σi has
a positive or zero real part, a nontransient solution exists.
Because an absorbing layer like the M-PML is designed to
dissipate all energy exiting the physical domain, asymptotic
stability for the M-PML is desired. Thus, for the purposes of
this work, we consider system (37) as stable if all the eigen-
values fσig have negative real parts; otherwise, the system is
unstable. In the next section we propose a simple procedure
to determine whether there are eigenvalues σi of A with a
positive or zero real part.

Now we turn our attention to classical PML for more
general orthotropic media. Those models not only exhibit
the instability due to multiple eigenvalues with zero real part
mentioned in the preceding discussion (polynomial growth;
see equation A.4 in the Appendix), but may also develop in-

considerably earlier during a simulation. We illustrate this
second type of instability with a few examples. In particular,

(59) and (60) depend on the ratio of the elastic coefficients

We have shown that with the simple eigenvalue sensi-
tivity analysis one can design robust M-PML terminations,
that is, media that are asymptotically stable. It should be
pointed out, however, that prediction of the sign of the real
part of the eigenvalues based on the sign of the first eigen-
derivative is valid only if the damping coefficients are small
(close to zero). If the damping coefficients are large enough
to allow the eigenvalues to oscillate and change sign as they
move away from the values that they have for zero damping,
then the analysis presented in the preceding discussion based
on the first derivatives is not valid any more. Such cases may
be encountered when the damping profiles of the M-PML are
specified to grow very fast, and much higher values of the
ratios p(cid:2)y=x(cid:3) and p(cid:2)x=y(cid:3) would be required to stabilize such
media. However, because very high damping ratio ampli-
tudes increase the reflections due to the numerical dispersion
that exists at the discrete level of the PML terminations (e.g.,
Collino and Tsogka, 2001), using small damping ratios, and
therefore, reasonably thicker M-PMLs for stabilization, seems
to be a preferable option.

Conclusions

A nonconvolutional, split-field perfectly matched layer,
referred to as the multiaxial perfectly matched layer, has been
presented. The new formulation is the result of the generali-
zation of the classical PML to a medium in which damping

profiles are specified in more than one direction. Under
the hypothesis of small damping coefficients, we proposed
an eigenvalue sensitivity analysis based on first derivatives
to provide effective criteria to study the stability of the
M-PML. With such a method we demonstrated that
the
stability of the M-PML can be related to the ratios of the
specified damping profiles, and thus we provided a general
procedure to construct robust, stable M-PML models for
anisotropic media.
